Rooftop solar is one of the few home upgrades that can pay for itself several times over. It can also disappoint if it's oversized, badly shaded or bought on optimistic assumptions. The difference comes down to five numbers you can estimate before you talk to anyone.

The five numbers that decide payback

  1. Installed cost per watt. Residential systems typically land between $2.50 and $3.50 per watt in North America before incentives, and often less in Australia and parts of Europe and Asia where installation markets are mature. Prices for the same system can vary 20–30% between installers, which is why comparing three quotes is the fastest way to improve your return.
  2. Annual yield (kWh per kW). A 1 kW array produces roughly 900–1,100 kWh a year in the UK, Germany or Canada's east; 1,300–1,500 in much of the US, southern Europe and India; and 1,600–1,900 in the US Southwest, Australia or the Middle East. Our solar estimator calculates this from a full year of satellite-modelled sunshine for your exact location.
  3. Self-consumption share. Each kWh you use on site saves the full retail price. A typical home without a battery uses 30–50% of its solar output directly; with daytime occupancy, a heat pump or an EV that charges at home, that can rise to 60–80%.
  4. Export value. Power you send to the grid earns whatever your utility credits — full retail under classic net metering, but frequently much less under newer "net billing" or export-tariff schemes.
  5. Electricity price and its trajectory. Higher prices mean faster payback. Over 25 years, even modest annual price growth adds substantially to lifetime savings.

A worked example

Take a home using 900 kWh a month at $0.18/kWh, in a location yielding 1,350 kWh per kW per year:

ItemValue
Annual use10,800 kWh
System to cover ~90%7.2 kW (17 × 430 W panels)
Production≈9,700 kWh/yr
Cost at $2.80/W≈$20,200
Savings (50% self-used at $0.18, rest exported at $0.09)≈$1,310/yr
Simple payback≈14 years

Now change one assumption at a time. Full net metering lifts savings to about $1,750 and payback to roughly 11 years. A 20% cheaper quote cuts payback by nearly three years. Adding an EV that soaks up midday power can push self-consumption past 70%. This sensitivity is the whole story: solar is rarely "worth it" or "not worth it" in the abstract — it depends on your tariff, your roof and your quote.

What about incentives?

Incentives change often, so treat any figure you read online as a starting point. In the United States, the 30% federal residential clean-energy tax credit (Section 25D) ended for systems placed in service after 31 December 2025, so payback there now depends more on state and utility programmes, net-metering rules and installer pricing. Many other countries, states and provinces offer rebates, low-interest loans, feed-in tariffs or VAT relief. Ask every installer to itemise the incentives they've assumed and check them against official sources.

Do you need a battery?

A battery earns its keep when the gap between what you pay for grid power and what you're paid for exports is large, when you have time-of-use pricing with expensive evening peaks, or when backup power has real value to you (frequent outages, medical equipment, a home business). Where full net metering still applies, a battery is mostly an insurance purchase. Read our home battery guide for the numbers.

When solar is a poor fit

  • Heavy, unavoidable shading from trees or buildings.
  • A roof that will need replacing within five years — replace it first.
  • Very low electricity prices combined with low export credits.
  • You expect to move within a few years and your local market doesn't value solar homes.

The carbon side

Panels typically repay the energy used to manufacture them within one to three years and then generate for 25–30 years or more. How much CO₂ they avoid depends on your grid: the dirtier it is, the bigger the benefit. Our estimator shows tonnes avoided using your country's grid intensity.
