Not all energy advice is equal. Turning off a phone charger saves cents; replacing a gas furnace can save tonnes. This list is grouped by cost and ordered roughly by impact within each group. Start with your footprint to see which categories dominate for you.

Free (do these this week)

  1. Lower the thermostat by 1–2 °C in winter (and raise it in summer). Heating and cooling are usually a home's largest energy use; each degree typically saves a few percent of heating energy.
  2. Wash laundry cold. Most of a washing machine's energy goes into heating water.
  3. Shift flexible loads to clean, cheap hours using a grid forecast and time-of-use tariff.
  4. Turn down the water-heater setpoint to around 50–55 °C where safe and permitted.
  5. Drive smoothly and check tyre pressure — aggressive driving and under-inflation raise fuel use noticeably.
  6. Air-dry when you can — tumble dryers are among the hungriest appliances.
  7. Switch off standby loads on game consoles, AV systems and old desktops with a smart strip.

Under $200

  1. Swap remaining bulbs for LEDs — they use roughly 75–85% less electricity than incandescents.
  2. Seal drafts around doors, windows and attic hatches with weatherstripping and caulk.
  3. Install a smart or programmable thermostat and actually set schedules.
  4. Low-flow showerheads cut hot-water use without a noticeable difference.
  5. Insulate hot-water pipes and older tanks.
  6. Thermal curtains for single-glazed or drafty windows.

$200–$5,000

  1. Professional energy audit with blower-door test — the best-value starting point for bigger upgrades.
  2. Attic and wall insulation — often among the fastest-payback upgrades in older homes.
  3. Heat-pump water heater — typically two to three times as efficient as a standard electric tank.
  4. Ductless mini-split for the most-used rooms, displacing oil, propane or baseboard heat.
  5. Level 2 EV charger with smart scheduling.
  6. Switch to a renewable electricity tariff or community solar subscription.
  7. Replace the oldest major appliance (often a fridge or freezer) with a high-efficiency model.

Big upgrades ($5,000+)

  1. Whole-home heat pump — the largest single carbon cut for most fossil-heated homes. Buyer's guide.
  2. Rooftop solar — typically pays back within a decade in good locations. Is it worth it?
  3. Electric vehicle at your next car replacement. The emissions maths.
  4. Home battery where tariffs reward it. Battery guide.
  5. Window replacement — comfortable and quieter, but usually the slowest payback; do insulation and air-sealing first.
