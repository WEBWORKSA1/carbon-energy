Short answer: across their full life, battery-electric cars produce substantially less CO₂ than comparable petrol or diesel cars on almost every grid in the world. Independent life-cycle studies — from the International Council on Clean Transportation, national laboratories and university groups — consistently find the EV advantage ranges from modest on coal-heavy grids to very large on clean grids. Here's how the maths works so you can test it for your own situation.

1. Manufacturing: the battery "debt"

Building an EV currently emits more than building an equivalent petrol car, mostly because of the battery. A common estimate puts the extra at roughly 3–7 tonnes of CO₂ for a mid-size car, depending on battery size and where the cells were made. This number is falling as factories switch to cleaner electricity and battery chemistry improves.

2. Driving: where the EV pays it back

A petrol car burning 7.5 L/100 km emits about 173 g CO₂ per km from the tailpipe alone (2.31 kg per litre), plus more for extracting and refining the fuel. An EV using 17 kWh/100 km emits 0.17 × your grid intensity grams per km:

3. The break-even distance

Divide the manufacturing debt by the per-km saving. With 5 t of extra manufacturing emissions and a 250 g/kWh grid, the saving is about 130 g/km, so the EV breaks even after roughly 38,000 km — two to three years of typical driving. On a 30 g/kWh grid it's under 30,000 km; on a 750 g/kWh grid it may take 100,000 km or more. Cars typically last 200,000–300,000 km, and grids get cleaner every year, so the lifetime advantage grows over time. Our EV vs petrol tool calculates this for your country, mileage and prices.

4. Common objections

"The electricity just comes from fossil fuels."

Partly, on many grids — but power plants convert fuel to motion far more efficiently than a car engine, and the share of fossil power keeps falling. Even on coal-heavy grids, studies generally still find a lifetime advantage, albeit smaller.

"Batteries need replacing every few years."

Real-world data shows most modern EV batteries retain the large majority of their capacity well beyond 150,000 km, and warranties commonly cover 8 years. End-of-life packs are increasingly reused for stationary storage or recycled.

"What about mining?"

Mining for lithium, nickel and cobalt has real environmental and social impacts that need strong standards. But a petrol car burns its own weight in fuel every couple of years, and that oil also has to be extracted, refined and shipped. Recycling can recover a high share of battery metals; fuel, once burned, is gone.
