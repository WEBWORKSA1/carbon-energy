Heat pumps are the single biggest lever most households have for cutting carbon: space and water heating are often the largest slice of a home's footprint, and a heat pump delivers that heat using a fraction of the energy of a boiler or furnace. They also cool in summer. But installation quality varies enormously, and a poorly designed system can be noisy, expensive to run and uncomfortable.

How they work, in one paragraph

A heat pump is a refrigerator run in reverse. A refrigerant absorbs low-grade heat from outdoor air (or the ground), a compressor squeezes it to raise its temperature, and that heat is released indoors through air handlers, radiators or underfloor pipes. Because it moves heat rather than creating it, a heat pump typically delivers 2.5–4.5 kWh of heat for every kWh of electricity — a coefficient of performance (COP) of 2.5–4.5. The seasonal average is called SCOP.

Do they work in cold climates?

Yes — modern cold-climate units keep producing useful heat well below −20 °C, and Nordic countries with harsh winters have some of the highest heat-pump adoption in the world. Efficiency does fall as it gets colder, so good designs either size the heat pump for the coldest typical day or pair it with a small backup (electric resistance or an existing furnace) for the few extreme hours each year.

Sizing: where most installs go wrong

An undersized heat pump leans on expensive backup heat; an oversized one short-cycles, wastes energy and wears out faster. Insist on a room-by-room heat-loss calculation (Manual J in North America, or the equivalent standard in your country) rather than "same size as your old furnace". Ask for the design outdoor temperature they've used and the expected output at that temperature.

Running costs vs gas

The economics hinge on the ratio between your electricity and gas prices. Divide the electricity price by the SCOP and compare it with the gas price divided by your boiler efficiency. Example: electricity at $0.18/kWh ÷ SCOP 3.2 = $0.056 per kWh of heat; gas at $0.07/kWh ÷ 0.90 = $0.078. The heat pump wins. Where electricity is more than about four times the price of gas per kWh, running costs can be similar or higher — though a time-of-use tariff or solar can change that. Carbon savings

On most grids a heat pump cuts heating emissions immediately — by 50–80% on moderate grids and by over 90% on hydro or nuclear-heavy grids like Québec, Norway or France — and the benefit grows every year as grids decarbonise.

Incentives

Many regions offer rebates, low-interest loans or utility programmes. In the US, the federal 25C tax credit ended for equipment placed in service after 31 December 2025; state and utility rebates may still apply. In Canada, the UK and the EU, programmes vary by province or country and change frequently — verify current terms before you sign.

Seven questions to ask every installer

  1. Will you do a room-by-room heat-loss calculation? Can I see it?
  2. What's the unit's output and COP at our design temperature?
  3. Where will the outdoor unit go, and what's its sound level?
  4. Do our ducts or radiators need upgrading for low flow temperatures?
  5. How will backup heat be controlled so it only runs when needed?
  6. What warranty covers parts, compressor and labour — and who services it?
  7. Which rebates have you included, and who files the paperwork?

Red flags

  • No heat-loss calculation, or sizing "by square footage".
  • Pressure to sign the same day.
  • Savings claims with no stated electricity and gas prices.
  • Vague answers on refrigerant handling or certification.
