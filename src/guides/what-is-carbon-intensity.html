The definition

Carbon intensity is the amount of greenhouse gas emitted to produce one kilowatt-hour (kWh) of electricity, usually expressed as grams of CO₂-equivalent per kWh (gCO₂e/kWh). A grid running entirely on coal sits around 800–1,000 g/kWh; a gas-fired grid around 400–500 g/kWh; and a grid dominated by hydro, nuclear, wind and solar can be below 50 g/kWh.

Because every electric appliance, heat pump and EV draws from the same grid, carbon intensity is the conversion factor that turns your kWh into kilograms of CO₂. A home using 700 kWh a month emits roughly 0.35 t of CO₂ a year on a 40 g/kWh grid — and more than 5.5 t on a 650 g/kWh grid. Same fridge, same habits, sixteen times the impact.

Rule of thumb: under 100 g/kWh is very clean, 100–250 low, 250–450 moderate, 450–650 high, and above 650 very high. See where your country sits on our country rankings.

Why it varies so much

Between countries

The national average reflects the generation mix built over decades. Countries with big rivers (Norway, Paraguay, Iceland), large nuclear fleets (France, Sweden) or abundant wind (Denmark, Uruguay) have very clean grids. Countries that industrialised on domestic coal — South Africa, Poland, India, Indonesia, Kazakhstan — have the dirtiest. Large federations hide wide internal variation: Québec and Manitoba run almost entirely on hydro, while Alberta has historically relied on fossil fuels; in the US, the Pacific Northwest and parts of the Northeast are far cleaner than the coal-heavy interior.

Hour by hour

Within a single grid, intensity moves constantly. Solar pushes it down at midday; still, cold evenings push it up as gas plants ramp to meet peak demand. On windy nights Britain's grid can dip below 50 g/kWh; on a calm winter evening it can exceed 250 g/kWh. Our live grid page shows this in real time with a 48-hour forecast.

Year by year

Grids are decarbonising fast. Wind and solar have grown from a few percent of global electricity in 2010 to well over a tenth today, and coal plants are closing across Europe and North America. That means the lifetime emissions of anything electric — a heat pump, an EV — fall every year it's in service, while a gas boiler or petrol car is locked in at the emissions of the day it was built.

Average vs marginal intensity

The average intensity is total emissions divided by total generation — the figure most calculators (including ours) use for annual footprints. The marginal intensity is the emissions of whichever plant responds when demand changes, usually a gas or coal unit. Marginal figures are useful for operational decisions — like when to charge a battery — and they explain why shifting load to sunny or windy hours matters even more than the average suggests. For reporting your annual footprint, use the average; for timing flexible loads, look at the forecast.

How to use it

  1. Know your number. Pick your country on our home page or open its profile.
  2. Time-shift flexible loads. Dishwashers, laundry, water heaters, EV charging and home batteries can all run in the cleanest window. This often cuts their emissions by a third or more — and with a time-of-use tariff, the cost too.
  3. Electrify where the grid is clean. On grids below ~450 g/kWh, a heat pump typically beats a gas boiler on emissions from day one; an EV beats a petrol car on almost every grid on Earth over its lifetime. Run the numbers in our tools.
  4. Generate your own. Rooftop solar displaces grid power at the times of day when solar is abundant. Estimate yours with the solar estimator.
  5. Buy clean power. A genuinely additional renewable tariff or community solar subscription can reduce your share further.

FAQ

Is carbon intensity the same as emissions?

No. Intensity is per kWh; emissions are intensity multiplied by how many kWh you use. A clean grid with huge demand can still emit a lot in total.

Do the figures include life-cycle emissions?

The country averages we show (from Ember via Our World in Data) are life-cycle figures, which include emissions from building plants and supplying fuel, so even wind and nuclear have small non-zero values.

Why does my utility report a different number?

Utilities often report the intensity of their own supply contracts rather than the physical grid, and may use different years or methods. Both can be valid — check which one your reporting standard requires.
