A home battery stores electricity — from your solar panels or from the grid when it's cheap or clean — and releases it when you need it. Whether it's a good investment depends on three things: your tariff structure, how much your solar exports earn, and how much you value backup power.

Three ways a battery earns money

  1. Solar self-consumption. Store midday surplus and use it in the evening instead of exporting it for a low credit and buying it back at full price. The value per kWh is the gap between your import price and your export credit.
  2. Time-of-use arbitrage. Charge from the grid at off-peak rates and discharge during expensive peaks — works even without solar.
  3. Grid services. Some utilities and retailers pay batteries to support the grid during peak events (often called virtual power plants).

And one way it earns peace of mind: backup power during outages, if the system includes the right switching hardware.

Sizing basics

  • Usable capacity (kWh) determines how long it can run. Many homes find 10–15 kWh covers evening and overnight use for essential loads.
  • Power (kW) determines what it can run at once. Heat pumps, ovens and EV chargers need high power.
  • Round-trip efficiency of 85–95% is typical for lithium-iron-phosphate (LFP) systems.
  • Warranty: look for 10 years and a stated energy throughput or cycle count.

Quick payback test

Annual value ≈ kWh shifted per day × 365 × (import price − export credit) × round-trip efficiency. Example: shifting 8 kWh/day with a $0.25 import price and a $0.06 export credit at 90% efficiency is worth about $500 a year. Divide the installed battery cost by that figure for a simple payback, then compare with the warranty period. If payback exceeds the warranty, the case rests on backup value or future tariff changes.

When a battery makes most sense

  • Export credits are low relative to import prices.
  • You're on a time-of-use or demand tariff with expensive peaks.
  • Outages are frequent or costly for you.
  • A virtual-power-plant programme pays for participation.

When to wait

  • You have full retail net metering — the grid already acts as a free battery.
  • Your electricity is cheap and flat-rate.
  • You're planning an EV with bidirectional charging that could serve the same purpose.
